Static fire to liftoff: how long does Starship actually take?

For every Starship flight from Flight 4 through Flight 14, this page lines up the final static fire of the booster, the final static fire of the ship, and the launch — then measures the gap that matters: the days from both vehicles done testing to liftoff. At the bottom, that history is turned into a prediction window for Flight 15.

How to read this — and the caveats

Why “both done” is the number that predicts

The booster-only gap swings wildly — 14 to 90 days across these flights — because the booster often fires weeks before the ship finishes (Flight 10's booster waited 81 days; Flight 9's, 54). Once both vehicles are done, the spread collapses: every flight from 6 onward launched 14–31 days later. Stacking, wet dress rehearsal, and final checks fill that window.

The two outliers aren't hardware stories

Flight 5 (79 days) sat stacked-ready while SpaceX waited on the FAA license — the gap was regulatory, not technical. Flight 4 (62 days) was the early program moving slower across the board. Neither is a good guide for Flight 15, which is why the predictor uses Flights 6–14.

Scrubs don't move the launch date used here

Several flights scrubbed a first attempt — Flight 13 at T−0, Flight 12 at a T−40 s hold, Flight 10 twice (a ground LOX leak, then weather), Flight 8 at T−40 s. The launch date in the table is the day the vehicle actually lifted off, so a scrub or two is already baked into these intervals.

What could still break the prediction

The historical window assumes testing finishes cleanly. It does not account for a failed or repeated Ship 42 static fire, a long stacking/WDR problem, weather, or the FAA license, which had not been posted as of Oct 26. One date also carries a small source disagreement: Flight 14's restack after its Sep 25 destack is pinned only to “by Sep 26–27,” which doesn't affect any interval.
